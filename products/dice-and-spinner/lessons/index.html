    Probability Dice & Spinner Lessons

    Ground probability theory in statistically valid empirical trials. These lessons guide students through frequency distributions, the Law of Large Numbers, and multi-event sample spaces.

    ← Back to Product Overview View Assembly Guide →

    Classroom Demonstration Video: Lessons 1 & 2

    Watch mass-balanced dice roll and the 10-sector needle spin to gather empirical classroom data.

    Curriculum Demonstration (Lessons 1 & 2) Law of Large Numbers • 18mm precision dice • Fair low-friction spinner
    ∅

    Why Physical Data Matters

    Students often struggle to distinguish theoretical probability (\(P = 1/6\)) from actual experimental outcomes. By conducting high-speed physical trials with unbiased instruments, students physically observe variance in small samples and convergence in large samples.

    Lesson 01 Target Grades: 6–8 • Standard: CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.7.SP.C.6

    The Law of Large Numbers: 10-Sector Frequency Convergence

    Lesson Objective: Students conduct 10-spin, 50-spin, and combined classroom 200-spin trials to observe how relative frequency converges toward theoretical probability \(P = 0.10\).

    Empirical Precision Instruments: 18 mm precision chamfered d6 die with centered pips paired with a low-friction balanced pointer needle. Fair Randomization

    Step-by-Step Classroom Investigation

    1.
    Determine Theoretical Probability: Inspect the 10-sector uniform dial (sectors labeled 0 through 9). Each sector spans an identical \(36^\circ\) angle. Calculate: \[ P(\text{Sector } 7) = \frac{1}{10} = 0.10 = 10\% \]
    2.
    Run a Small Sample Trial (N = 10): Spin the needle 10 times. Record tallies on a frequency table. Calculate the experimental frequency: e.g., if 7 appeared 3 times, relative frequency is \(\frac{3}{10} = 30\%\). Note the expected high variance.
    3.
    Expand to Medium Sample (N = 50): Spin 40 more times (total 50). Recalculate relative frequencies. Notice how extreme spikes begin to flatten.
    4.
    Combine Classroom Data (N = 250+): Pool data across 5 lab groups. Plot a bar graph on the board. The bars will form a remarkably uniform distribution, proving that large sample sizes smooth out short-term noise.

    Δ Key Misconception Diagnostic: "The Gambler's Fallacy"

    The Common Error: If the needle lands on Sector 3 three times in a row, students claim "Sector 3 is hot" or conversely "Sector 3 cannot land again because it's due for a different number."

    The Physical Fix: The spinner has no mechanical memory. The aerodynamic needle starts every spin independently. Prove this by showing that \(P(\text{Sector } 3)\) on spin #4 remains exactly \(\frac{1}{10}\).

    ✓ Independent Transfer Check

    Prompt: If a student spins the 10-sector dial 500 times, approximately how many times would you expect it to land on an even number \(\{0, 2, 4, 6, 8\}\)? Explain your reasoning.

    Lesson 02 Target Grades: 7–10 • Standard: CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.7.SP.C.8

    Compound Events: Die Roll + Spinner Dial Sample Spaces

    Lesson Objective: Model two-stage independent compound probability events by rolling a mass-balanced die alongside spinning a sector dial, constructing tree diagrams and coordinate grids.

    Step-by-Step Classroom Investigation

    1.
    Enumerate the Compound Sample Space:
    • Die outcomes: \(\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}\) (6 outcomes)
    • Spinner 4-Color outcomes: \(\{\text{Red}, \text{Blue}, \text{Green}, \text{Yellow}\}\) (4 outcomes)
    • Total sample space size: \(6 \times 4 = 24\text{ equally likely compound outcomes}\).
    2.
    Calculate Compound Probability: Find \(P(\text{Roll } > 4\text{ AND Spin Red})\): \[ P(\text{Roll } \in \{5, 6\}) = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}, \quad P(\text{Red}) = \frac{1}{4} \] \[ P(\text{Compound}) = \frac{1}{3} \times \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{12} \approx 8.33\% \]
    3.
    Conduct Empirical Verification: Perform 36 trials, rolling the die and flicking the spinner simultaneously. Count occurrences of \((5, \text{Red})\) and \((6, \text{Red})\) to verify experimental alignment with the theoretical \(\frac{1}{12}\).

    Δ Key Misconception Diagnostic: "Adding Instead of Multiplying"

    The Common Error: Adding independent event probabilities: \(\frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{5}{12}\).

    The Physical Fix: Draw a \(6 \times 4\) grid on paper. Each square represents one outcome. Shading only the matching squares shows clearly that only 2 out of 24 boxes qualify, proving that compound probability multiplies.

    ✓ Independent Transfer Check

    Prompt: Draw a tree diagram showing all possible outcomes of rolling a fair die and spinning a 10-sector dial. How many total outcomes exist? What is the probability of rolling an odd number and spinning a prime number?
